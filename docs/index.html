How to Properly Do Resonance Breathing

Resonance breathing is different from most popular breathing exercises. Instead of holding your breath or forcing long counts, the goal is to breathe smoothly at the slow individual pace (~4.5 to 6.5 breaths per minute) where your heart rate swings most freely—rising softly as you breathe in and dropping as you breathe out.

It takes practice, but it should not take effort. Just slide into the rhythm.

Most of us normally breathe 12 to 18 times a minute, so slowing down to 5 or 6 breaths per minute feels strange at first. Do not feel discouraged if your first session feels slightly awkward—give yourself a few quiet sessions to get used to it. Once your nervous system trusts the pace, you stop working at it and simply ride the wave.

Four Simple Habits While You Practice

1

Keep it continuous—no breath holds

Follow the smooth inhale / exhale wave cleanly—40:60 by default (for example ~4.2 s in and ~6.4 s out at 5.65 BPM), or adjusted between 50:50 and 25:75 in settings. Turn gently from breathing in to breathing out and back without pausing (even brief breath holds spike chest pressure and interrupt the wave).

2

Breathe softly through your nose into your belly

Let the air drift quietly in through your nose so your belly rises gently. Leave your upper chest, shoulders, and neck loose and still.

3

Release the exhale gently without pushing

Let the air escape slightly slower than you breathed in—either through softly pursed lips (as if keeping a candle flame flickering without blowing it out) or quietly through your nose. Do not force all the air out.

4

Take shallow, normal breaths—not deep yoga breaths

Only inhale roughly your normal resting air volume, stretched calmly across a slower breath. Taking huge breaths at this pace quickly blows off too much CO₂ and works against relaxation.

Setting Up a Calm Session

Pick a genuinely peaceful spot (not your commute)

This is not meant for walking around or during your commute. For a calibration test, sit upright and still in a really comfortable chair. For daily practice, sitting comfortably or lying down in bed works nicely so you do not have to move a finger.

Hide the clock if numbers create pressure

Watching your heart rate jump up and down or watching seconds tick down can subtly keep you alert instead of relaxed. Once you settle into the sound cue or pace, look away—or use the “Hide live HR & countdown” option below.

Start comfortable, stay continuous

Your circulation takes ~1–3 minutes of steady slow breathing to lock into resonance. Many studies use 15–20 minutes once or twice daily, yet starting with 5–10 minutes (or turning on the watch’s optional 30–120 s free-breathing halfway break) keeps you relaxed while building familiarity.

Helpful times in everyday life

Good moments include unwinding after exercise once your pulse has come back down, settling down in the evening before bed, or calming nerves before stressful events or races.

Listening to Your Body & Quick Troubleshooting

What You Notice Why It Happens What Helps Right Away
Mild dizziness, lightheadedness, or tingling You are breathing slowly, yet inhaling too deeply and blowing off too much CO₂, which briefly narrows cerebral blood vessels. Immediately take noticeably quieter, shallower breaths—just your normal resting air volume—or pause for 30–60 s of ordinary breathing.
Air hunger, tight jaw, or neck/shoulder tension Trying too hard, actively pushing air out, holding breath briefly, or picking a cadence slightly slower than feels easy today. Drop your shoulders, loosen your jaw, let the exhale release passively, or nudge the pace up by 0.1–0.5 BPM.
Jumpy heart rate or noisy test readings Dry chest strap contacts, arm movement, talking, or brief pauses at inhalation/exhalation turnarounds. Moisten strap electrodes well, settle comfortably still, and keep inhale/exhale transitions smooth and continuous.

When Not to Practice (Safety Note)

This app and website are for general wellness and breathwork practice only, not a medical device. Avoid slow paced breathing if you wear a cardiac pacemaker (whose fixed pacing overrides natural heart rate oscillations) or have severe metabolic conditions such as metabolic acidosis (e.g., advanced kidney disease or untreated diabetes), where rapid breathing helps balance blood acidity. Consult a doctor first if you have heart, lung, or blood pressure conditions, and stop anytime you feel unwell.

How the Garmin Watch App Helps

On your watch, Peak Frequency can both measure where your resonance cadence sits today and guide calm daily practice sessions offline.

1. Personal Starting Anchor

If height and biological sex are stored in your Garmin profile, the app computes an initial cadence using the Hasuo et al. regression formulas, clamped to 4.5–6.5 BPM and rounded to 0.1 BPM (falling back to 175 cm / male, or a manual anchor from 4.0 to 8.5 BPM). Because height and sex only explain roughly half of the variance between people, the app treats this strictly as a starting anchor.

2. Two-Round Calibration Test

Starts with 2 minutes of natural resting breathing, then runs Round 1 (center, +0.25, −0.25 BPM) and Round 2 (+0.125, −0.125, center around the Round 1 winner)—each 2 minutes paced followed by 2 minutes rest (~26 minutes for both rounds, or stop after Round 1). Each paced stage drops the first ~60 s as settling time, filters beat-to-beat artifacts, and scores both swing amplitude and phase alignment near 0°. If two rates score nearly equal, trust whichever feels calmest and easiest.

3. Gentle Daily Practice (5–20 Min)

Pick 5, 10, 15, or 20 minutes, an optional halfway free-breathing break (30–120 s), and manual cadence (3.5–7.0 BPM). Cues rely on quiet vibration by default (beeps optional) and short breathing reminders so you can close your eyes. An accelerometer check softly warns “Stay still” on the watch if sustained arm movement occurs.

4. Chest Strap vs. Optical HR & Privacy

Reads beat-to-beat R–R intervals whenever an HRV chest strap is paired, and falls back to regular heart rate if none arrives within 15 s. A chest strap is strongly recommended for the calibration test because optical wrist sensors smooth fast changes. Everything runs 100% locally with zero internet permission; sessions save locally as .FIT breathwork activities if you choose.

For the Curious: Why It Works & Links to the Science

Your breath is the only part of your autonomic control loop that you can guide consciously at any moment. Knowing briefly why it works can make slow, gentle breathing feel much less mysterious.

Why a healthy heart is not a metronome (HRV)

Even at a steady 60 beats per minute, your heart does not beat once every 1.000 s like a quartz clock. Intervals naturally shift from beat to beat (for instance 820 ms, then 940 ms, then 790 ms). That beat-to-beat fluctuation is Heart Rate Variability (HRV), reflecting the balance between your sympathetic “gas pedal” (alert effort) and your parasympathetic vagus nerve “calm brake” (rest and recovery).

A. Breath & heart rate coupling (RSA)

Whenever you inhale, vagal braking softens slightly and heart rate edges upward. Whenever you exhale, vagal signaling returns and heart rate drops. Physiologists call this natural wave Respiratory Sinus Arrhythmia (RSA).

B. The ~5-second blood pressure delay

Meanwhile, pressure sensors (baroreceptors) in your aorta and carotid arteries watch blood pressure changes. Because blood travels through vascular pathways with fluid inertia, it takes roughly ~5 seconds per half-cycle for pressure adjustments to feed back through the brainstem.

C. The playground swing effect & why 6.0 BPM is only an average

Think of the cardiovascular system like a child on a playground swing:

Everyday pace (~12–18 BPM)

Breathing every 3–5 seconds pushes out of step with the ~5-second blood pressure feedback timing, so heart rate swings stay small and irregular.

Resonance pace (~4.5–6.5 BPM / ~0.075–0.11 Hz)

When one half-breath takes roughly 5 seconds, exhale begins right as the baroreflex signals your heart to slow down. Breath and heart rate align near 0° phase synchrony while blood pressure swings opposite (~180°), amplifying smooth HRV waves naturally.

Because adult vascular length and blood volume vary (taller people and men sit slightly slower on average; shorter individuals and women slightly faster), 6.0 BPM is only a rough population average. Shifting cadence by just 0.5 BPM can noticeably shrink that oscillation—and posture, fatigue, or tension can still nudge early readings slightly.
